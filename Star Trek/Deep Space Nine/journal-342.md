# DS9 S6E26 — "Tears of the Prophets" (550.txt)

**Written by:** Ira Steven Behr & Hans Beimler | **Directed by:** Allan Kroeker | **Entry:** 342

## Summary

The episode opens in the Bajoran Shrine, the Gratitude Festival wrapping up. Kira is still angry with Odo for arresting a Vedek who was fundraising without a license. Odo tries to explain; Kira won't hear it. In the Wardroom, Admiral Ross awards Sisko the Christopher Pike Medal of Valor — and then tells him he's been chosen to plan the invasion of Cardassia. The operation's target: the Chin'toka System, a weak point Damar inadvertently exposed by pulling ships to the Romulan front. But Damar has installed orbital weapon platforms protected by regenerative shields and powered by a central generator on a moon.

Dax and Worf are privately discussing having a baby. Bashir and Quark are morose about it — both harbored feelings for Dax. Vic Fontaine, ever the sage, tells them the truth sets you free.

On Cardassia Prime, Dukat returns — not as a supplicant but as a man possessed. He brings a Bajoran artifact containing a Pah-wraith. Weyoun dismisses him as a madman. Damar listens. Dukat channels the Pah-wraith into his own body, emerging with blood-red eyes.

The Prophets appear to Sisko, insisting he not go to Cardassia. Ross forces the issue: Emissary or Starfleet captain. Sisko chooses captain. The fleet departs — the Defiant carrying Sisko, Kira, Worf, O'Brien, Garak, Nog, and journalist Jake.

The battle is catastrophic. The weapon platforms activate precisely when the fleet arrives. O'Brien finds the power generator on a moon. Sisko, reeling from a psychic blow (the wormhole's destruction), is led off the bridge by Jake. Kira takes command. O'Brien tricks the platforms into firing on their own power source. The grid goes dark. The ground invasion begins.

But the wormhole is gone.

On the station, Dax visits the Bajoran shrine to pray for her baby with Worf. Dukat materializes, eyes burning red. He drains the life from her with the Pah-wraith's power, then releases the entity into the Orb of Contemplation, destroying it — and the wormhole — from within. Dax's body slams to the floor. Dead.

Back on DS9, Bashir saved the Dax symbiont. Jadzia is gone. Worf's scream echoes through the infirmary. He chants the Klingon death prayer over her body.

Sisko, shattered by guilt, tells Jadzia's coffin he should have listened to the Prophets. He takes a leave of absence. Jake goes with him. Kira notices: his desk is empty. He took the baseball.

Final image: Sisko in New Orleans, sitting on his father's back steps in a cook's whites, scrubbing clams in the dark. Calm. Peaceful. Broken.

## Themes

### The Death That Wasn't Supposed to Happen

Jadzia Dax's death is the most consequential death in DS9's run — and possibly the most controversial. Behind the scenes, Terry Farrell was effectively forced out when the producers offered her only a reduced role for season 7. The death is written as noble sacrifice (sort of), but it reads differently when you know the production context: a beloved character killed because of contract negotiations.

But within the text of the episode, the death lands with extraordinary weight. Jadzia isn't killed in battle — she's murdered in a shrine while praying for a baby. The violation is not just physical but spiritual. The Bajoran shrine, the one place the Prophets might be found, becomes the site of their desecration. Dukat doesn't just kill Dax; he kills the connection between Bajor and the Prophets *through* Dax's death.

### Dukat's Descent into Pah-Wraith Possession

This is the point of no return for Gul Dukat. Everything he's been — the conflicted Cardassian, the grief-stricken father, the power-hungry schemer — collapses into something new. The Pah-wraith possession is a clever narrative choice because it reframes Dukat's entire arc: his megalomania, his obsession with Bajor, his need to be worshipped. The Pah-wraiths are the Prophets' dark mirror — they want worship, not relationship. Dukat finds his true spiritual home.

The prayer Dukat chants is Bajoran, but the vessel he breaks open reveals a Pah-wraith, not a Prophet. The implication is that Dukat has been studying Bajoran texts and deliberately chose the path of the dark gods. This isn't madness so much as *conversion* — Dukat has become a zealot for the wrong religion, and it gives him a coherence his former self never had.

### The Prophets' Warning

The wormhole aliens appear to Sisko in the forms of those around him — Worf, Martok, Ross, Letant — each insisting he belongs on Bajor. They speak in their characteristic non-linear temporality, but the warning is clear: if Sisko leaves, something irreparable will happen. He ignores them. The consequence is the wormhole's destruction and Dax's death.

This creates a paradox that the show doesn't fully resolve: if the Prophets exist outside linear time, they *knew* Sisko would ignore them. Their warning becomes less about prevention and more about *witnessing*. "We told you" is the only vindication beings outside time can offer when linear beings make tragic choices. It echoes "The Visitor" — Jake's futile attempts to change the past — but this time the tragedy is permanent.

### Ross's Ultimatum

Admiral Ross forces the choice that Sisko has been avoiding for six years: Emissary or Starfleet captain. You cannot be both. The ultimatum is reasonable from a military perspective — a commander cannot have divided loyalties — but it's brutally unfair from a spiritual one. The Prophets chose Sisko before Starfleet did. Ross is asking Sisko to renounce his identity at the moment it matters most.

Sisko chooses Starfleet. The Prophets are silent. The wormhole dies. The choice is correct by every military metric — the invasion succeeds — and catastrophic by every spiritual one. "Tears of the Prophets" is the proof that sometimes there is no right answer, only wrong ones with good intentions.

### The Baseball

Sisko's baseball is the show's most consistent symbol across seven seasons. It appears in "Emissary" (the pilot) — a gift from his father, a reminder that "you can't predict the outcome of a baseball game." It sits on his desk through the entire series. In "The Visitor," the alternate-future Sisko has it in his New Orleans apartment. And now, at the end of this episode, Sisko takes it with him. The empty desk is the void where command used to be. The baseball is the one thing he keeps.

Kira's quiet recognition — "He took it with him" — is one of the most emotionally devastating lines in the series.

### Vic Fontaine as Accidental Prophet

Vic sings "Here's to the Losers" for Bashir and Quark, telling them they lost Dax a long time ago. "The truth shall set you free." There's a bitter irony: Vic the hologram offers better emotional counsel than the actual Prophets do in this episode. Sometimes wisdom comes from the things we made, not the things that made us.

## Cross-Reference

The wormhole's destruction mirrors the collapse of an internal connection — the void when something that structured your reality is suddenly gone. Sisko's guilt ("I should have listened") is the grief of someone who was warned and chose differently anyway. This episode resonates with the Flask Framework's panel about permeable edges: the Prophets gave Sisko a boundary, he crossed it, and the consequence was final. But the episode also shows what comes after: Sisko, in civilian clothes, scrubbing clams. Not healing. Not rebuilding. Just... doing the next thing. The mundane as survival.

**What from this slot matters beyond this slot?** "Tears of the Prophets" is the price of divided identity — the moment when choosing one face means losing the world the other face gave you access to. The wormhole was a relationship, not a resource. Treating it as the latter because the military demanded it broke something that can't be rebuilt. The lesson is the one the Prophets tried to teach: some paths are chosen by being, not by rank.
